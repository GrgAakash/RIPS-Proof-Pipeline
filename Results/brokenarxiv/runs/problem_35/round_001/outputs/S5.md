3. Work on C3

Let G be a minimal counterexample to the target theorem, and let R=R(G).

If R=G, then G is solvable. By C2, G contains a nilpotent subgroup H with |H| >= k(G), contradicting that G is a counterexample. Thus a minimal counterexample cannot be solvable.

It remains to handle the intended case 1 < R < G. Put Q=G/R. Since R is the full solvable radical, R(Q)=1. By C5, Q contains a nilpotent subgroup \bar P with |\bar P| >= k(Q). Let P be the inverse image of \bar P in G. Then P/R is nilpotent, hence solvable, and R is solvable, so P is solvable.

If P=G, then Q is nilpotent. Since Q also has trivial solvable radical, Q must be trivial, contradicting R<G. Hence P<G.

By C2, P contains a nilpotent subgroup M with |M| >= k(P). However, the supplied tools do not give k(P) >= k(G), nor do they give a way to lift the nilpotent subgroup \bar P of G/R together with a nilpotent subgroup of R to a nilpotent subgroup of G large enough to dominate k(G).

The standard inequality k(G) <= k(R)k(G/R), even if available, is insufficient by itself: nilpotent subgroups in R and G/R do not generally combine into a nilpotent subgroup of G of product order. For example, in S3 with R=C3 and G/R=C2, the product size would be 6, but S3 has no nilpotent subgroup of order 6.

Therefore C3 cannot be completed from the stated local dependencies alone.

4. Proof of C6

Assume C2, C3, and C5.

Suppose the target theorem is false, and choose a counterexample G of minimal order. The trivial group is not a counterexample, since k(1)=1 and the trivial subgroup is nilpotent of order 1.

Let R=R(G). If R>1, then C3 contradicts minimality. Hence R=1. But then C5 applies directly and gives k(G) <= m(G), again contradicting that G is a counterexample.

Thus no finite counterexample exists, and the theorem follows.
