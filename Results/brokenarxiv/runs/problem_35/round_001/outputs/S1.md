2. Subproof or failure

Let \(\operatorname{Cl}(G)\) denote the set of conjugacy classes of a finite group \(G\), so \(k(G)=|\operatorname{Cl}(G)|\).

**Lemma 1: direct products.** If \(A\) and \(B\) are finite groups, then
\[
k(A\times B)=k(A)k(B).
\]

Proof. For \((a,b)\in A\times B\) and \((x,y)\in A\times B\),
\[
(x,y)(a,b)(x,y)^{-1}=(xax^{-1},yby^{-1}).
\]
Hence the conjugacy class of \((a,b)\) in \(A\times B\) is exactly
\[
a^A\times b^B.
\]
Thus conjugacy classes of \(A\times B\) are in bijection with ordered pairs consisting of one conjugacy class of \(A\) and one conjugacy class of \(B\). Therefore \(k(A\times B)=k(A)k(B)\). By induction, \(k(G_1\times\cdots\times G_r)=\prod_i k(G_i)\) for any finite direct product.

**Lemma 2: quotient class inequality.** If \(N\trianglelefteq G\), then
\[
k(G/N)\leq k(G).
\]

Proof. Let \(\pi:G\to G/N\) be the quotient map. For \(g\in G\),
\[
\pi(g^G)=(gN)^{G/N}.
\]
Thus every conjugacy class of \(G/N\) is the image of at least one conjugacy class of \(G\). Hence the induced map from \(\operatorname{Cl}(G)\) onto \(\operatorname{Cl}(G/N)\) is surjective, so \(k(G/N)\leq k(G)\).

**Lemma 3: classes inside a normal subgroup.** If \(N\trianglelefteq G\), then the number of \(G\)-conjugacy classes contained in \(N\) is at most \(k(N)\).

Proof. Since \(N\trianglelefteq G\), conjugation by elements of \(G\) permutes the elements of \(N\), and it sends \(N\)-conjugacy classes to \(N\)-conjugacy classes. A \(G\)-conjugacy class contained in \(N\) is a union of \(N\)-conjugacy classes. Distinct \(G\)-classes give disjoint unions of \(N\)-classes. Therefore the number of such \(G\)-classes is at most the number of \(N\)-classes, namely \(k(N)\).

**Lemma 4: coset orbit bound.** If \(N\trianglelefteq G\) and \(t\in G\), then the number of \(N\)-orbits on the coset \(tN\), under conjugation by \(N\), is at most \(k(N)\).

Proof. By Burnside’s orbit-counting formula, the number of \(N\)-orbits on \(tN\) is
\[
\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{n\in N}|\{x\in tN: nx=xn\}|.
\]
Fix \(n\in N\). If \(\{x\in tN: nx=xn\}\) is nonempty and \(x_0\) lies in it, then this set is exactly \(x_0C_N(n)\). Indeed, if \(c\in C_N(n)\), then \(x_0c\) centralizes \(n\). Conversely, if \(x\in tN\) centralizes \(n\), then \(x_0^{-1}x\in N\), and since both \(x_0\) and \(x\) centralize \(n\), we have \(x_0^{-1}x\in C_N(n)\). Thus the fixed-point set has size either \(0\) or \(|C_N(n)|\). Therefore the number of \(N\)-orbits on \(tN\) is at most
\[
\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{n\in N}|C_N(n)|.
\]
Applying Burnside’s formula to the conjugation action of \(N\) on itself, this last quantity is \(k(N)\). Hence the claimed bound follows.

**Lemma 5: normal-subgroup class-count inequality.** If \(N\trianglelefteq G\), then
\[
k(G)\leq k(N)k(G/N).
\]

Proof. Partition the conjugacy classes of \(G\) according to the conjugacy class of \(G/N\) to which they map. Fix a conjugacy class \(\overline C\) of \(G/N\), and choose \(t\in G\) such that \(\overline C=(tN)^{G/N}\).

Every \(G\)-conjugacy class mapping to \(\overline C\) intersects the coset \(tN\): if \(gN\in \overline C\), then some conjugate of \(g\) has image \(tN\), hence lies in \(tN\). Inside \(tN\), each \(N\)-orbit under conjugation is contained in a single \(G\)-conjugacy class. Therefore the number of \(G\)-classes mapping to \(\overline C\) is at most the number of \(N\)-orbits on \(tN\), which is at most \(k(N)\) by Lemma 4.

There are \(k(G/N)\) choices for \(\overline C\). Summing the bound over all quotient conjugacy classes gives
\[
k(G)\leq k(N)k(G/N).
\]

In particular, since \(k(N)\leq |N|\), one also has
\[
k(G)\leq |N|\,k(G/N).
\]

**Lemma 6: nilpotent subgroups and products.** Subgroups and finite direct products of nilpotent groups are nilpotent.

Proof. Use the standard central-series definition: a group \(X\) is nilpotent if it has a finite central series
\[
1=X_0\trianglelefteq X_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq X_c=X
\]
with \(X_i/X_{i-1}\leq Z(X/X_{i-1})\) for every \(i\).

If \(H\leq X\) and \(X\) has such a central series, set \(H_i=H\cap X_i\). Then
\[
1=H_0\trianglelefteq H_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq H_c=H,
\]
and \(H_i/H_{i-1}\leq Z(H/H_{i-1})\), because commutators of elements of \(H\) with elements of \(H_i\) lie in \(H\cap X_{i-1}=H_{i-1}\). Thus \(H\) is nilpotent.

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are nilpotent with central series \((X_i)\) and \((Y_j)\), then \(X\times Y\) has a central series obtained by first lifting the series for \(X\) as \(X_i\times 1\), then adjoining the series for \(Y\) as \(X\times Y_j\). Each successive factor is central in the relevant quotient, so \(X\times Y\) is nilpotent. Induction gives the finite direct-product case.

**Lemma 7: central lifting of nilpotence.** Let \(Z\leq Z(X)\). If \(X/Z\) is nilpotent, then \(X\) is nilpotent. More generally, if \(Z\leq Z(X)\), \(Y\leq X\), \(Z\leq Y\), and \(Y/Z\) is nilpotent, then \(Y\) is nilpotent.

Proof. It is enough to prove the second statement, since the first is the case \(Y=X\).

Let
\[
1=Q_0\trianglelefteq Q_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq Q_c=Y/Z
\]
be a central series for \(Y/Z\). Let \(Y_i\) be the inverse image of \(Q_i\) in \(Y\). Then
\[
Z=Y_0\trianglelefteq Y_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq Y_c=Y.
\]
Since \(Z\leq Z(Y)\), the series
\[
1\trianglelefteq Z\trianglelefteq Y_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq Y
\]
is central: the first factor \(Z/1\) is central in \(Y\), and for \(i\geq 1\), the centrality of \(Q_i/Q_{i-1}\) in \((Y/Z)/Q_{i-1}\cong Y/Y_{i-1}\) says exactly that \(Y_i/Y_{i-1}\leq Z(Y/Y_{i-1})\). Hence \(Y\) is nilpotent.

This proves C1.
