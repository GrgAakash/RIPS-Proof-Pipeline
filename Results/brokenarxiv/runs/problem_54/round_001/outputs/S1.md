2. Subproof or failure

Let \(u\in C^2(\mathbb R^N)\), \(u>0\), solve
\[
-\Delta u=|x|^\alpha u^p,\qquad u(x)=O(|x|^{2-N})\quad (|x|\to\infty).
\]

**Scaling.** For \(\lambda>0\), define
\[
u_\lambda(x)=\lambda^{(N-2)/2}u(\lambda x).
\]
Then
\[
-\Delta u_\lambda(x)
=\lambda^{(N-2)/2+2}|\lambda x|^\alpha u(\lambda x)^p
=\lambda^{(N+2)/2+\alpha-(N-2)p/2}|x|^\alpha u_\lambda(x)^p.
\]
Since \((N-2)p=N+2+2\alpha\), the exponent of \(\lambda\) is \(0\). Thus \(u_\lambda\) solves the same equation. Also
\[
p+1=\frac{2(N+\alpha)}{N-2},
\]
so both \(\int |\nabla u|^2\) and \(\int |x|^\alpha u^{p+1}\) are scaling-invariant whenever finite.

**Decay and integrability.** Since \(u\in C^2(\mathbb R^N)\) and \(u(x)=O(|x|^{2-N})\), \(u\) is globally bounded. For large \(r=|x|\),
\[
|x|^\alpha u^p=O(r^{\alpha-(N-2)p})
=O(r^{-N-2-\alpha}),
\]
and
\[
|x|^\alpha u^{p+1}
=O(r^{\alpha-(N-2)(p+1)})
=O(r^{-2N-\alpha}).
\]
Near \(0\), \(u\) is bounded and \(|x|^\alpha\) is locally integrable. Hence
\[
|x|^\alpha u^p\in L^1(\mathbb R^N),\qquad
|x|^\alpha u^{p+1}\in L^1(\mathbb R^N).
\]

By the interior gradient estimate for Poisson equations, applied on balls \(B_{r/4}(x)\) for \(r=|x|\) large,
\[
|\nabla u(x)|
\le C\left(r^{-1}\sup_{B_{r/4}(x)}|u|+r\sup_{B_{r/4}(x)}|x|^\alpha u^p\right)
=O(r^{1-N}).
\]

Therefore, as \(R\to\infty\),
\[
\int_{\partial B_R}|u\,\partial_\nu u|\,dS=O(R^{2-N})\to 0,
\]
\[
R\int_{\partial B_R}|\nabla u|^2\,dS=O(R^{2-N})\to 0,
\]
and
\[
R^{\alpha+1}\int_{\partial B_R}u^{p+1}\,dS=O(R^{-N-\alpha})\to 0.
\]

Multiplying the equation by \(u\) on \(B_R\) gives
\[
\int_{B_R}|\nabla u|^2\,dx
=\int_{B_R}|x|^\alpha u^{p+1}\,dx
+\int_{\partial B_R}u\,\partial_\nu u\,dS.
\]
Letting \(R\to\infty\),
\[
\int_{\mathbb R^N}|\nabla u|^2\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R^N}|x|^\alpha u^{p+1}\,dx<\infty.
\]

**Kelvin transform.** Define, for \(x\ne0\),
\[
v(x)=|x|^{2-N}u\left(\frac{x}{|x|^2}\right).
\]
The standard Kelvin identity gives
\[
-\Delta v(x)=|x|^{-N-2}(-\Delta u)\left(\frac{x}{|x|^2}\right).
\]
Writing \(y=x/|x|^2\), so \(|y|=|x|^{-1}\), we get
\[
-\Delta v
=|x|^{-N-2}|y|^\alpha u(y)^p
=|x|^{-N-2-\alpha}\bigl(|x|^{N-2}v(x)\bigr)^p
=|x|^\alpha v^p,
\]
again because \((N-2)p=N+2+2\alpha\).

The decay of \(u\) gives \(v(x)=O(1)\) as \(x\to0\). Since \(|x|^\alpha v^p\) is locally bounded near \(0\), the removable singularity theorem for bounded solutions of Poisson equations implies that \(v\) extends across \(0\) as a \(C^2\) solution of
\[
-\Delta v=|x|^\alpha v^p.
\]
The strong maximum principle gives \(v>0\). Also, since \(u\) is bounded near \(0\), \(v(x)=O(|x|^{2-N})\) as \(|x|\to\infty\). Thus the Kelvin transform preserves the solution class.

**Pohozaev identity.** Because \(|x|^\alpha\) may fail to be \(C^1\) at \(0\) when \(0<\alpha<1\), integrate first over the annulus \(A_{\varepsilon,R}=B_R\setminus \overline{B_\varepsilon}\). For \(x\ne0\),
\[
x\cdot\nabla(|x|^\alpha)=\alpha |x|^\alpha.
\]
Using the standard Pohozaev divergence identity,
\[
\operatorname{div}\left(\frac12|\nabla u|^2x-(x\cdot\nabla u)\nabla u\right)
=(x\cdot\nabla u)(-\Delta u)+\frac{N-2}{2}|\nabla u|^2,
\]
and
\[
|x|^\alpha u^p(x\cdot\nabla u)
=\frac1{p+1}|x|^\alpha x\cdot\nabla(u^{p+1}),
\]
one obtains
\[
\frac{N-2}{2}\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}|\nabla u|^2
-\frac{N+\alpha}{p+1}\int_{A_{\varepsilon,R}}|x|^\alpha u^{p+1}
=
\int_{\partial A_{\varepsilon,R}}
\left[
\frac12(x\cdot\nu)|\nabla u|^2
-(x\cdot\nabla u)\partial_\nu u
-\frac1{p+1}(x\cdot\nu)|x|^\alpha u^{p+1}
\right]dS.
\]
The inner boundary terms vanish as \(\varepsilon\to0\) because \(u,\nabla u\) are bounded near \(0\) and \(\alpha>0\). The outer boundary terms vanish as \(R\to\infty\) by the estimates above. Hence
\[
\boxed{
\frac{N-2}{2}\int_{\mathbb R^N}|\nabla u|^2\,dx
=
\frac{N+\alpha}{p+1}\int_{\mathbb R^N}|x|^\alpha u^{p+1}\,dx.
}
\]
Since \(p+1=2(N+\alpha)/(N-2)\), this is the critical Pohozaev balance.

**Spherical-harmonic legitimacy.** For every \(r>0\), \(\theta\mapsto u(r\theta)\) is \(C^2(S^{N-1})\). Therefore it has an \(L^2(S^{N-1})\) spherical-harmonic expansion
\[
u(r,\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty\sum_m a_{\ell m}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta),
\]
where
\[
-\Delta_{S^{N-1}}Y_{\ell m}=\ell(\ell+N-2)Y_{\ell m}.
\]
The coefficients \(a_{\ell m}(r)\) are \(C^2\) for \(r>0\), obtained by differentiating under the integral. Thus later angular spectral decompositions are analytically legitimate.
