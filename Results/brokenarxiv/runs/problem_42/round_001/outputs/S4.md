2. **Assigned Subproblem**

Prove the delicate step: given nonzero `r`, an atomic `s`, and atomic `rs`, prove `r` is atomic without assuming unique factorization.

3. **Prerequisite Subclaims Needed**

To make this step valid, one would need a cancellation-type subclaim such as:

> If `s` and `rs` are atomic and `R` is IDF, then `r` is atomic.

This is not a standard immediate consequence of IDF. In particular, from a factorization of `s` and a factorization of `rs`, one cannot cancel atoms one by one unless atoms are prime or the domain has some stronger factorization property. IDF only says finitely many atom-divisors occur up to associates; it does not say irreducibles divide products as primes do.

4. **Attempted Proof and Obstruction**

Let `s` be atomic and `rs` atomic. If `s` is a unit, then `r` is associate to `rs`, so `r` is atomic.

Otherwise, write

```text
s = u q_1 ... q_m
rs = v p_1 ... p_n
```

with units `u, v` and atoms `q_i, p_j`.

Since `s | rs`, each `q_i` divides `rs`. But in a general integral domain, an atom dividing a product of atoms need not divide any individual factor. Therefore one cannot conclude that some `q_i` is associate to some `p_j`, and so one cannot remove the atoms of `s` from the factorization of `rs`.

IDF does not repair this gap directly: it gives only finiteness of atom-divisors of each nonzero element, not primality, refinement, or cancellability of factorizations.
