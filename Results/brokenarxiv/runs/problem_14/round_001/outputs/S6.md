1. Let \(F\) be a fixed graph containing at least one cycle, and put
\[
m=\operatorname{ex}(n,F).
\]
By definition, \(h_F(n,1)\) is the minimum number of copies of \(F\) among all \(n\)-vertex graphs with \(m+1\) edges.

2. The upper bound
\[
h_F(n,1)\le c(n,F)
\]
is immediate. Indeed, any graph counted in the definition of \(c(n,F)\) is obtained by adding one edge to an extremal \(n\)-vertex \(F\)-free graph, hence has \(m+1\) edges. Therefore this class of graphs is a subclass of the admissible class in the definition of \(h_F(n,1)\). Taking the minimum over the larger admissible class gives \(h_F(n,1)\le c(n,F)\).

3. [KEY STEP] To prove the reverse inequality by the S0 route, one needs the following structural assertion: for all sufficiently large \(n\), if \(G\) minimizes the number of copies of \(F\) among all \(n\)-vertex graphs with \(m+1\) edges, then there exists an edge \(e\in E(G)\) contained in every copy of \(F\) in \(G\).

S2 explicitly leaves it unsolved and identifies the invalid shortcut: after deleting an edge from \(G\), the resulting graph has exactly \(m=\operatorname{ex}(n,F)\) edges, but this edge count alone does not imply that the graph is \(F\)-free. The definition of \(\operatorname{ex}(n,F)\) says only that every \(F\)-free \(n\)-vertex graph has at most \(m\) edges; it does not say that every graph with exactly \(m\) edges is \(F\)-free.

5.
