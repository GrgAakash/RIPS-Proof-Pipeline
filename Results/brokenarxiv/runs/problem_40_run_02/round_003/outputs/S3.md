1. **Assigned Claim**

Assume there are constants `C > 0` and `M0` such that for all `M >= M0`,

\[
0<R_M\le C(M+1)2^{-M},
\]

where

\[
R_M=r-\sum_{m=1}^M m\epsilon_m2^{-m}.
\]

Then

\[
R_M\to 0
\]

and therefore

\[
r=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m\epsilon_m2^{-m}.
\]

2. **Proof That \(R_M\to 0\)**

It is standard that

\[
(M+1)2^{-M}\to 0
\]

as \(M\to\infty\), since exponential decay dominates linear growth. For example, for all sufficiently large \(M\),

\[
M+1\le 2^{M/2},
\]

so

\[
(M+1)2^{-M}\le 2^{-M/2}\to 0.
\]

Thus, from

\[
0<R_M\le C(M+1)2^{-M},
\]

the squeeze theorem gives

\[
R_M\to 0.
\]

3. **Exact Convergence To \(r\)**

Let

\[
S_M=\sum_{m=1}^M m\epsilon_m2^{-m}.
\]

By definition of \(R_M\),

\[
R_M=r-S_M,
\]

so

\[
S_M=r-R_M.
\]

Since \(R_M\to 0\), it follows that

\[
S_M\to r.
\]

Therefore the infinite series converges, and its value is exactly

\[
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m\epsilon_m2^{-m}=r.
\]

4. **Convergence Justification**

A series converges precisely when its sequence of partial sums converges. Here the partial sums \(S_M\) converge to \(r\), so the series converges.

If additionally, as in the intended subset construction, \(\epsilon_m\in\{0,1\}\), then absolute convergence also follows from

\[
0\le m\epsilon_m2^{-m}\le m2^{-m}
\]

and the standard convergence of \(\sum_{m\ge1}m2^{-m}\). But this comparison is not needed for exact convergence, since the remainder bound already forces \(S_M\to r\).
