1. Assignment restatement

Check, locally and explicitly, that the intended bounded-degree construction is order-sensitive: it can distinguish \((a,b)\) from \((b,a)\). Also explain why the forbidden construction \(g=x_1x_2\) in a commutative ring cannot represent arbitrary nonsymmetric binary functions.

2. Subproof or failure

Let \(j:X\to R\) be injective, and suppose the construction uses ordered-pair selector polynomials
\[
S_{a,b}(x_1,x_2)
\]
with the Kronecker property
\[
S_{a,b}(j(u),j(v))=
\begin{cases}
1 & (u,v)=(a,b),\\
0 & (u,v)\neq(a,b).
\end{cases}
\]
Then the construction distinguishes ordered inputs immediately: if \(a\neq b\), then
\[
S_{a,b}(j(a),j(b))=1,\qquad S_{a,b}(j(b),j(a))=0.
\]
Thus the evaluation data for \((a,b)\) and \((b,a)\) are different, even though \(R\) is commutative. The asymmetry is carried by the two variable slots and by the ordered selector index \((a,b)\), not by noncommutative multiplication.

This avoids the forbidden symmetric collapse. Indeed, if one instead tried to use only
\[
g(x_1,x_2)=x_1x_2
\]
in a commutative ring, then for all \(a,b\in X\),
\[
g(j(a),j(b))=j(a)j(b)=j(b)j(a)=g(j(b),j(a)).
\]
So any represented function would satisfy
\[
j(f(a,b))=j(f(b,a)).
\]
Since \(j\) is injective, this forces
\[
f(a,b)=f(b,a).
\]
Therefore \(x_1x_2\) can only represent symmetric binary functions. The selector-based construction does not have this defect, because it supplies separate degree-\(\le 2\) terms for \((a,b)\) and \((b,a)\).
